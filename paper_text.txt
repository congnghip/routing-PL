ABSTRACT
The recent surge in Vietnam’s e-commerce has significantly strained urban infrastructure, leading to increased
last-mile delivery times, higher transportation costs, and diminished customer satisfaction. In response, many
countries are adopting sustainable solutions like parcel lockers, which facilitate contactless deliveries. However,
integrating parcel lockers introduces a new variant to the classic routing problem: the Vehicle Routing Problem
with Parcel Lockers (VRPPL), which demands effective management of multiple delivery options. To address
this, this research proposes a novel 3D Enhanced Parallel Ant Colony Optimization (3D-PACO) model. Our core
contribution is the introduction of a pioneering multidimensional pheromone matrix to extend the traditional
Ant Colony Optimization (ACO) for the VRPPL’s multiple delivery options. Further enhancing this, we propose
an adaptive mechanism for dynamic ant allocation per thread for economical resource utilization. Finally, we
adopt a master–slave parallel schema to the VRPPL enabling deployment of a large total number of ants. The
experimental results on various VRPPL benchmark datasets indicate statistically significant improvements in
solution quality with tremendous savings in computational runtime of 46.4% to 83.5% across different dataset
sizes, compared to the original work.

1. Introduction
To mitigate the urban infrastructure strain, parcel lockers have been
considered as a sustainable green solution, and have been adopted
in many countries, including China, England, Poland, etc. [1]. However, the integration of parcel lockers, or pickup and drop-off (PUDO)
points in general, brings new challenges to the current routing problem. Unlike traditional vehicle routing problems (VRP) in which each
customer is associated with a unique delivery point, there can be
multiple alternative locations for a customer’s request. Furthermore,
these delivery options might be preoccupied or capacity-limited. Such
challenges demand the routing algorithm not only be able to find nearoptimal permutations of customers to be served but also consider the
route options.
Ant Colony Optimization (ACO), proposed by Dorigo [2], is a wellestablished method to solve VRPs, inspired by the foraging behavior of
ants. In the ACO system, pheromone trails serve as indirect, distributed
numeric communication channels between ants. Our research identifies
a gap in the literature where the multidimensional pheromone matrix,
which can directly model multiple delivery options for each customer,

remains largely unexplored, therefore, explaining the sparsity of research implementing ACO to solve VRPPL or VRPDO. Furthermore,
since adding multiple dimensions increases the search space, sequential
ACO models could suffer from expensive computation and memory
issues, especially for large-scale instances.
Parallel computing involves distributing computational tasks across
multiple processors to reduce execution time, leveraging architectures
like multicore CPUs, GPUs, and distributed systems such as Apache
Hadoop and Spark [3]. The integration of parallel computing with
metaheuristics aims to enhance efficiency, scalability, and solution
quality for large-scale problems, addressing limitations of sequential
implementations. The inherent population-based nature of ACO makes
it inherently suitable for parallel processing [4]. The algorithm operates
with multiple sub-colonies of artificial ants, each of which independently explores the solution space. This independence allows for the
distribution of the tasks associated with each sub-colony, such as solution construction and evaluation, across multiple processing elements,
enabling concurrent execution.

This research offers three key contributions. First, we propose a
novel 3D pheromone matrix that significantly extends the ACO model’s
applicability to VRPs featuring multiple delivery options, explicitly
incorporating customer service preferences as constraints. This ACO
model is further enhanced with a local search mechanism to effectively
escape local optima. Second, we implement a master–slave parallel
architecture designed to efficiently deploy a large population of ants.
An elitist update strategy not only reduces pheromone synchronization
runtime at the master process but also enhances exploitation, leading

adjusting the number of ants per thread. This significantly enhances
the model’s robustness and ensures economical computational resource

2. Literature review

2.2. Common approaches to VRPs

2.1. Different variants of VRP

Several techniques have been developed to tackle various routing
problems and are commonly categorized as exact methods, heuristics,
and metaheuristics. Early approaches to the VRP relied on exact optimization techniques such as Mixed-Integer Programming formulations
solved via column generation [10] or branch-and-cut algorithms [11].
Despite guaranteeing optimality, exact methods become computationally expensive as problem size grows. Heuristic methods, like the Savings Algorithm [12] and Nearest Neighbor [13], deliver good-quality
solutions quickly but are highly dependent on the problem structure
and prone to being trapped in local optima.
Meta-heuristic approaches provide a powerful framework for overcoming the limitations of traditional optimization methods for the
VRP, guiding the design of effective search strategies that can escape
local optima. As classified by Stamadianos et al. [14], these methods fall into two primary categories: Population Search and Local
Search. Population Search methods maintain and iteratively update a
pool of candidate solutions; this class includes evolutionary algorithms
(e.g., Genetic Algorithms) and swarm-based algorithms such as Particle
Swarm Optimization (PSO) [15], Artificial Bee Colony (ABC) [16],
Cuckoo Search [17] and ACO. Local Search methods, including 2-opt
and Tabu Search, intensify the search around a single solution using
neighborhood moves.
Despite numerous advancements in meta-heuristic and machine
learning, the VRPPL remains a challenging problem due to its complex characteristics. A key bottleneck is the lack of efficient mapping
mechanisms to translate customer permutations into delivery options,
which is crucial for improving solution quality. Yu et al. [5] introduces
probabilistic assignment based on parameter 𝑝, and nearest neighbor heuristic insertion for the initial solution. While the advantage
of this method relies on its simplicity and model-agnostic nature,
it suffers from a significant limitation: the mapping mechanism is
not adaptive. This static nature requires extensive tuning for 𝑝 and
can lead to suboptimal solutions. Moreover, the stochastic nature of
the method can result in significant variation between runs. Nguyen
et al. [9] propose a two-level allocation algorithm, which employs the
assignment-first, routing-second mechanism. However, similar to the
approach by Yu et al. [5], this two-level mechanism also struggles to
address the intricate relationship effectively between delivery options
and the corresponding optimal routes (see Fig. 2).

Capacitated VRP (CVRP) is one of the most extensively studied
variants. CVRP requires that each customer can only be serviced by one
vehicle, and the total demand of the customers assigned to a vehicle
should not exceed its capacity. Additionally, there can be constraints
related to the number of used vehicles or the maximum traveling
distance of each one. Vehicle Routing with Time Windows (VRPTW)
introduces additional complexity by assigning a specific time window
during which each customer must be serviced. While early arrivals are
allowed, they incur waiting times, and late arrivals are penalized. If
considered as soft constraints, traveling time along with distance are
both targets to be optimized in multi-objective problems [6].
Recent studies have expanded the classic VRP by incorporating
multiple delivery options, such as home and locker delivery, and by
addressing new challenges posed by parcel lockers. For instance, Tilk
et al. [7] introduced the Vehicle Routing Problem with Delivery Options (VRPDO), where customers can choose between home delivery
or locker delivery. Grabenschweiger et al. [8] proposed the Vehicle
Routing Problem with Heterogeneous Locker Boxes (VRPHLB), which
models the heterogeneous sizes and capacities of the lockers. The problem becomes intricate as it requires efficient routing and parcel-packing
strategies to ensure that parcels are stored within locker constraints.
Despite reflecting real-world logistics with locker heterogeneity and
parcel sizing, the research’s model lacks preference flexibility.
The Vehicle Routing Problem with Parcel Lockers (VRPPL), introduced by Yu et al. [5], further extends these models by introducing
three types of customers: those requesting home delivery (type-1),
those requesting locker delivery (type-2), and those who are flexible
and can accept either option (type-3). The VRPPL model incorporates
time windows and vehicle capacities, while minimizing the total travel
distance. The authors extended Solomon’s VRPTW instances to create
new benchmark datasets for the VRPPL.
The crowdsourcing problem of [9] exhibits similarity with VRPPL
due to the incorporation of alternative delivery options and customer
types. The authors address these delivery options through an
Allocation-Routing Algorithm (ARA). Specifically, type-2 and type3 customers are assigned to their closest alternative delivery points
using K-means. Type-1 customers are handled by a two-level allocation
algorithm. Their results demonstrate that total distribution costs can be
reduced by recommending customers utilize these alternative delivery
points.

A key advantage of ACO for the VRPPL, when compared to other
swarm-based metaheuristics, is its constructive nature and explicit
incorporation of heuristic desirability. In this context, desirability is
defined as the inverse of the distance between delivery locations.
This feature allows ACO to effectively consolidate customers served
by the same locker, even during the initial search stages, without
heavy reliance on learned pheromone trails. As demonstrated in Fig.
3, since the distance between consecutive demands delivered to the
same locker is approximately zero (a small value is added to the
denominator to prevent division by zero), the desirability value 𝜂𝑖𝑗
becomes exceptionally high. This inherent mechanism efficiently guides
ants to create compact, consolidated routes from the outset.
An extensive body of research [18] has explored numerous extensions and variants of ACO for addressing both classical routing
problems and their increasingly complex modern extensions. The first
ACO algorithm is Ant System (AS) [2], followed by Ant Colony System [19] which introduces the indirect pheromone communication.
Despite initial encouragement, it was not proven to be competitive
against other state-of-the-art methods at that time. Elitist strategy
or Elitist Ant System (EAS), mentioned in the same paper, extends
AS by only allowing the best solutions to update pheromone trails;
such solutions can be in within the current iteration or global-best.
 −  Ant System () can be considered as a further
improvement of EAS, introducing a limitation interval for pheromone
value [𝜏𝑚𝑖𝑛 , 𝜏𝑚𝑎𝑥 ] ∀𝜏𝑖𝑗 to balance between exploration and exploitation.
Recent advancements in ACO frequently involve hybridization with
other metaheuristics and the incorporation of adaptive strategies. For
instance, Ren et al. [20] proposed a hybrid approach where ACO constructs initial solutions, which are then enhanced with adaptive tabu
operators, and global pheromone is updated using Simulated Annealing
(SA) as a replacement for elitist ants. Similarly, Wan et al. [21] leverage
SA to refine solutions generated by elite ACO ants, aiming to escape
local optima. Stodola et al. [22] integrate an adaptive pheromone
evaporation rate, dynamically adjusted based on information entropy,
to maintain population diversity. Similarly, Xue [23] employs an Adaptive ACO (ADACO) for the Multi-Depot Vehicle Routing Problem with
Time Windows (MDVRPTW), where the evaporation rate dynamically
decreases with iteration count. Finally, Zhou et al. [24] model the
Traveling Salesman Problem (TSP) as a Reinforcement Learning (RL)
framework and implement Stochastic Gradient Descent (SGD) as an
adaptive learning strategy for ACO.

3. Methodology
3.1. Mathematical model
The Vehicle Routing Problem with Parcel Lockers (VRPPL) is formulated following the original model of Yu et al. [5] without further
extensions. As shown in Table 1, in this problem, a homogeneous fleet
of vehicles operates from a central depot to serve customer demands
either at their homes or at parcel locker stations. The delivery network
is represented as an undirected graph 𝐺 = (𝑁, 𝐴), where 𝑁 is the set
of nodes and 𝐴 is the set of edges between nodes.
Customers are divided into three types:
1. Home delivery customers (type 1) who must receive deliveries
at home,
2. Locker delivery customers (type 2) who must receive deliveries
at a parcel locker,
3. Flexible customers (type 3) who can receive deliveries either
at home or at a parcel locker.

2.4. Parallel computing in ACO
Parallel Ant Colony Optimization (PACO) has proven effective for
solving large-scale combinatorial optimization problems such as the
VRP. Pedemonte et al. [25] provided an extensive taxonomy of parallel ACO implementations, detailing diverse schemas such as master–
slave (coarse-grain, medium-grain, and fine-grain), multi-colony, cellular, parallel independent runs, and hybrid models, each characterized by distinct communication and interaction mechanisms. ACO can
be parallelized across various architectures, including multicore-SIMD
CPUs [26], cloud computing environments [27], and GPUs [28,29].
One of the sub-categories of master–slave schema is coarse-grain,
which assigns complete solution construction and evaluation tasks
to individual slave processes, which periodically communicate their
results back to a master process that maintains global pheromone
information [30]. Its straightforward implementation has shown robust
scalability and reduced computational overhead, making it particularly suitable for large-scale VRP instances. Furthermore, shared memory [31] suggests effectiveness in managing global resources between
master and slaves, not only reduces communication overhead but also
the computational memory required.

Parcel lockers are subject to time windows for service and predefined service durations. Each vehicle has a maximum load capacity
and must start and end its route at the depot. The objective is to
minimize the total travel distance while satisfying vehicle capacity and
time-window constraints.

be annotated by 𝑖 for departure node or 𝑗 for the destination node.

To represent delivery-option effectiveness, the 2D pheromone matrix is extended to a three-dimensional tensor: 𝜏𝑖𝑗𝑜 , 𝑖, 𝑗 ∈ 𝑁𝐶 , 𝑜 ∈ 𝑂.
The 3D matrix introduces 𝑂 as a set the alternative delivery options
(e.g., home delivery, locker 1, locker 2, . . . ). Each element 𝜏𝑖𝑗𝑜 measures
the desirability for the vehicle 𝑘 to travel from the customer 𝑖 and

delivery options (home delivery and nearest locker delivery), we can
simplify 𝑂 to be 0 if home delivery is chosen and 1 if locker delivery is
selected. A key advantage of the ACO family in comparison with other
swarm-based metaheuristics is its decision interpretability, using the
pheromone density, as shown in Fig. 5. The brighter color represents
the higher density, thus indicating a higher probability that the ant
agent will choose such an arc.

Within each iteration, the solutions produced by all worker processes are asynchronously generated and then synchronously aggregated upon their completion. A key mechanism for managing convergence is the stagnation iteration counter (𝐼𝑠𝑡𝑎𝑔 ) which is incremented
whenever the current iteration fails to discover a new global best solution. Following this aggregation, an elitist strategy is employed: only
the top-𝑡 ants, identified by their optimal objective values, are permitted to update the global pheromone matrix. By restricting pheromone
deposition to this elite subset, our model promotes effective exploitation of the search space while significantly reducing the computational
cost associated with pheromone synchronization.
Finally, by utilizing a shared-memory architecture for the global
pheromone matrix, the algorithm eliminates the need to distribute
copies of the matrix to slave processes. This significantly reduces both
memory complexity from 𝑂(𝑚 × |𝑁|2 × |𝑂|) to 𝑂(|𝑁|2 × |𝑂|) and the
overhead associated with inter-process communication.

3.3. Solution representation
We denote each demand by the pair (𝑖, 𝑜), where 𝑖 ∈ 𝑁𝐶 identifies the customer and 𝑜 ∈ 𝑂 specifies the chosen option. Routes
are constructed incrementally using a Sequential Insertion Heuristic
(SIH). Beginning at the depot (𝑖 = 0), we select the next demand
(𝑗, 𝑜) according to the transition probability defined in Eq. (17). Upon
inserting each new demand into the route, we update the vehicle’s load
and the current time. This insertion process continues until adding any
remaining demand would violate either the vehicle’s capacity or the
prescribed time windows. At that point, the route is deemed saturated,
and we initiate a new route by resetting both load and time, modeling
parallel vehicle deployments.
As illustrated in Fig. 1, any consecutive demands for that locker are
aggregated into a single, larger demand. Even though the mathematical
model introduced by Yu et al. [5] allows multiple revisits to the same
locker by a vehicle, such an action is unlikely to discover more optimal
routes. Based on the assumption of the original work, a vehicle will
perform consolidated drop-off when visiting a locker. Consequently,
upon revisiting without returning to the depot, the vehicle will incur
extra mileage for a second trip and consume more of the time budget
due to the locker’s service time, despite having no packages. As a
result, to limit the search space to more promising regions and save
the computational runtime, the heuristic strategy of this work treats
revisits to the same locker after servicing an intervening location
as violations, and hence a new vehicle will be deployed. Finally, if
unsatisfied demands remain once all vehicles have been deployed, the
overall solution is declared infeasible.

4.4. Sensitivity analysis
Next, we employed a One-Factor-At-a-Time (OFAT) approach to analyze the impact of parameter variance across different datasets. Using
the BO-tuned parameter set as our baseline, we systematically varied
each parameter within a predefined range while keeping all other
parameters fixed. The percentage deviation 𝑃 𝐷 (%) was recorded for
each variation, reflecting the parameter’s influence on the model’s performance. For computational efficiency, 5 instances were randomly selected from each dataset, and the experiment was conducted five times
on each instance. The performances were aggregated by averaging the
results across these runs.
We began with the two parameters that most directly control the
search budget and sampling effort: the number of ants per thread (𝑚)
and the maximum allowed stagnation iterations (𝐼). Across all instance
sizes, these parameters show the clearest trade-off between runtime
and solution quality: increasing either 𝑚 or 𝐼 raises runtime in an
approximately linear manner while providing diminishing returns in
solution cost beyond a moderate threshold (see Fig. 7). In practice,
this means that modest populations and iteration budgets are sufficient
to capture most of the obtainable improvement, whereas overly small
values can be harmful. On our medium (see Fig. 8) and large tests (see
Fig. 9), we observed dramatic quality degradation when 𝐼 was set too
low (costs doubled when 𝐼 < 5 on medium and when 𝐼 < 10 on large
instances), and some cost increases occurred when 𝑚 dropped below
about 30 on large instances. Thus, when tuning for larger problems, it
is safer to increase 𝑚 and 𝐼 moderately rather than to scale them down
to reduce runtime; the observed thresholds are useful starting points
for a problem-dependent calibration.
The pheromone-heuristic balance, encapsulated by 𝛼 (pheromone
importance) and 𝛽 (heuristic importance), exhibits markedly different
behaviors: 𝛼 has strong, dataset-dependent effects, while 𝛽 is comparatively inert within the ranges we tested. Specifically, very low
pheromone influence (𝛼 ≤ 0.75) caused severe runtime penalties and
poorer quality on larger instances. Furthermore, runtime increases of
roughly 50% to 150% and cost rises of the order of 50% were observed
on medium/large sets, and on the large set the runtime curve is nonmonotonic with a local minimum near 𝛼 = 1.25. This indicates that
pheromone strength interacts with problem scale and search stability:

large (100 customers, 5 lockers). Each size includes 56 testing instances
across three distribution types: clustered (C), random (R), and randomclustered (RC). The locker time window is identical to the depot’s, and
the service time at a locker is half of a customer’s. A key assumption
made in this study is that lockers have infinite capacity, as the provided

The detailed computational benchmark results of Section 4.6 are

increases runtime with modest benefit. Thus, the sensible rule is to use
inexpensive or lightweight LS variants more aggressively and reserve
heavy LS for occasional refinement of elite solutions.
